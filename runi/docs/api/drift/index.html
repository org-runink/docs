      runi/drift

      runi/drift

      import "github.com/org-runink/runi/drift"

      Package drift tells you when a stream of measurements has changed regime.

      It is for series read from a running system over and over — a pressure stall percentage every second, a latency per request, a queue depth per scrape — where the question is “has this moved?” and the answer is needed as each reading arrives. Every type does O(1) work and holds fixed memory per update, allocates nothing, and is a plain value, so one detector per series is affordable for thousands of series.

      Which to use:

      • Welford and EWMA describe. Welford is the exact running mean and variance, numerically stable and mergeable across shards; EWMA is a smoothed level that forgets. Neither decides anything. Welford over a known-good stretch is how to get the target and σ the detectors need.
      • EWMAChart catches small shifts that persist — half a standard deviation, sustained — that a check on raw values never sees, and is a good general choice when the size of the shift you care about is unknown.
      • CUSUM catches a shift of a size you name, and for that size it is the fastest detector there is at a given false-alarm rate. It also estimates when the shift began (Since).
      • PageHinkley is CUSUM for when you have no target: the reference level is the series’ own running mean.
      • Decayed is not a detector. It turns a cumulative counter (time stalled, bytes sent) read at irregular times into a decayed rate — the “average over the last 10/60/300 s” that operating systems publish — which is usually the series you then want to watch.

      The detectors return a Signal: Up, Down or None, for the sample just added. A signal is a statistic crossing a threshold you chose; what follows from it is your code’s decision, and the detector carries on accumulating until you call Reset.

      c, _ := drift.DefaultCUSUM(target, sigma) for x := range readings { if s := c.Add(x); s != drift.None { log.Printf("%s shift began %d samples ago", s, c.Since()) c.Reset() } }

      Every type skips NaN and ±Inf rather than folding them in, and counts them: a missed reading should not poison a statistic for the rest of the series, and a series that is mostly NaN should be visible as one (Skipped). None is safe for concurrent use; give each goroutine its own, or guard it.

      What this package does not do

      It does not choose thresholds. The defaults offered (DefaultCUSUM, DefaultEWMAChart) are the textbook values, with the false-alarm rates the textbooks give for them, stated in their docs and checked by tests. Whether a false alarm every 465 samples suits a series read every second is a question about your system, not about statistics.

      It does not know about seasons. A daily cycle is, to every detector here, a shift up every morning and down every night. Remove the cycle first — the residual from github.com/org-runink/runi/season is the series to watch — or compare against the same hour of previous days yourself.

      It is univariate. Each detector watches one series. Shifts that show only in the relationship between series (two latencies that normally move together diverging) need a multivariate chart such as Hotelling’s T² or MEWMA, which this package does not provide.

      Signals are not probabilities. A CUSUM over h says the evidence crossed h; it does not say “99% sure”. The in-control average run lengths in the docs are the honest way to read the rate of false alarms, and they assume independent samples: on autocorrelated data every detector here signals more often than its documented rate. Model the autocorrelation away first (github.com/org-runink/runi/arimax residuals) if it is strong.

      It never acts. Nothing here alerts, throttles, restarts or retries; it returns numbers.

      CUSUM 

      type CUSUM struct

      CUSUM is the two-sided tabular cumulative-sum test for a shift in the mean away from a known target.

      Each side accumulates the evidence for a shift in its direction and is floored at zero, so that time spent in control is not credited against a later shift:

      S⁺t = max(0, S⁺{t−1} + x_t − target − k) S⁻t = max(0, S⁻{t−1} + target − x_t − k)

      and a side signals once it exceeds h. k is the slack: deviations smaller than k drain the sum rather than feed it. Set to half the shift you want to catch, the test is the fastest possible detector for a shift of exactly that size among those with the same false-alarm rate, in the worst-case sense of Lorden (G. V. Moustakides, “Optimal stopping times for detecting changes in distributions”, Annals of Statistics 14(4), 1986). For shifts much smaller than 2k it is slow, and EWMAChart is the better choice when the size is unknown.

      Since estimates when the shift began: a side becomes positive at about the sample the shift started, so the run of samples for which it has been positive is the age of the shift. This is Montgomery’s estimate, and it costs nothing to keep.

      Method: E. S. Page, “Continuous inspection schemes”, Biometrika 41(1/2), 1954. Tabular form, the Since estimate and the run-length tables quoted below: D. C. Montgomery, Introduction to Statistical Quality Control, Wiley, chapter on the cumulative sum control chart.

      k and h are in the data’s units. A signal does not reset the statistics: they keep accumulating, and keep signalling while the shift lasts, until you call Reset. Whether to reset, re-estimate the target, or simply note the change is the caller’s decision. Like EWMAChart, it assumes independent samples with a known in-control mean.

      The zero value is not usable; build one with NewCUSUM or DefaultCUSUM. Safe to copy, not safe for concurrent use. NaN and ±Inf are skipped and counted in Skipped. Each side is capped at the largest finite float64, so no finite input, however large, can turn the statistics into Inf or NaN.

      Defined in drift/cusum.go.

      Decayed 

      type Decayed struct

      Decayed turns a cumulative counter, read at irregular times, into an exponentially decayed average rate.

      Many system statistics are published as running totals rather than rates: microseconds spent stalled on memory or I/O since boot, bytes sent, requests served. What a reader wants is “the share of time stalled over the last minute” or “requests per second lately”, which is what operating systems report alongside such totals as 10, 60 and 300 second averages. Decayed computes that from the totals alone. For a counter of microseconds stalled, the rate is microseconds per second, so dividing by 1e6 gives the share of time (multiply by 100 for a percentage).

      Each Update takes the rate over the interval since the last one, r = Δtotal/Δt, and blends it into the average with

      e = exp(−Δt/window), a = a·e + r·(1−e).

      This is exact for irregular sampling: it is the continuous-time exponential average with time constant window of a rate that was r throughout the interval, which is all a pair of counter readings can say about it. A fixed per-sample weight, as in EWMA, would be wrong under jitter: a reading delayed to 3 s would count the same as one taken after 1 s, and a burst of readings close together would drag the average towards whatever those moments happened to show. Here a reading’s weight is the time it covers.

      After one window of a new constant rate the average has moved 1 − 1/e, about 63%, of the way from the old rate to the new one, whatever the spacing of the readings; after three windows, 95%. The first rate initialises the average directly rather than being blended into an implicit zero, which would read as a quiet system for the first window and then as a rise that never happened.

      A total lower than the previous one means the counter was reset, or wrapped past its maximum. With one reading the two cannot be told apart, so both are treated as a reset: the reading becomes a new baseline, no rate is produced, and Resets is incremented. A counter that wraps loses one interval, never yields an absurd rate.

      Time comes only from the at argument; nothing here reads the clock, so it is as testable as the caller’s clock is. Intervals are taken with time.Time.Sub, which uses the monotonic reading that time.Now attaches, so a wall-clock step between two such readings does not distort the interval. Readings at or before the previous one (a clock stepped backwards, a duplicate) are skipped and counted in Skipped, and leave the baseline where it was: the next valid reading covers their interval.

      Method: the exponential smoothing of irregularly spaced data of D. J. Wright, “Forecasting data published at irregular time intervals using an extension of Holt’s method”, Management Science 32(4), 1986, here applied to the interval-average rate, which makes the step response exact rather than approximate.

      The zero value is not usable; build one with NewDecayed. Safe to copy, not safe for concurrent use.

      Defined in drift/decayed.go.

      EWMA 

      type EWMA struct

      EWMA is an exponentially weighted moving average: each new value moves the average a fixed fraction alpha of the way towards itself,

      v_t = v_{t−1} + alpha·(x_t − v_{t−1}),

      so a value k samples old carries weight alpha·(1−alpha)^k. It describes a level; it does not test one. For a decision about whether the level moved, use EWMAChart, which adds the control limits this lacks.

      The first value initialises the average rather than being blended with an implicit zero, so a series that starts at 500 does not spend its first few dozen samples climbing up from nothing. The average counts samples, not time: for readings at irregular times, Decayed is the right tool.

      Method: the exponentially weighted average as used in forecasting by R. G. Brown, Statistical Forecasting for Inventory Control, McGraw-Hill, 1959.

      The zero value is not usable (its alpha is 0); build one with NewEWMA or NewEWMAHalfLife. Safe to copy, not safe for concurrent use. NaN and ±Inf are skipped and counted in Skipped.

      Defined in drift/ewma.go.

      EWMAChart 

      type EWMAChart struct

      EWMAChart is an exponentially weighted moving average control chart: it signals when a smoothed series leaves a band around a target level.

      The statistic is

      z_t = λ·x_t + (1−λ)·z_{t−1}, z_0 = target,

      and the band at sample t is

      target ± L·σ·sqrt( λ/(2−λ) · (1 − (1−λ)^(2t)) ).

      σ times the square root is the exact standard deviation of z_t when the x_t are independent with standard deviation σ. It starts at λσ and widens towards the asymptotic σ·sqrt(λ/(2−λ)); using the asymptotic band from the first sample would leave the chart nearly blind to a shift present from the start.

      Use it for small shifts that persist. Averaging over about 2/λ samples lets it see a shift of half a standard deviation that a chart of raw values would never flag, at the price of reacting more slowly than a raw-value check to a single large spike. For a shift of one known size CUSUM is the faster detector; the two have similar performance across a range of sizes.

      Method: S. W. Roberts, “Control chart tests based on geometric moving averages”, Technometrics 1(3), 1959. Exact-variance limits and run-length tables: J. M. Lucas and M. S. Saccucci, “Exponentially weighted moving average control schemes: properties and enhancements”, Technometrics 32(1), 1990. Presentation follows D. C. Montgomery, Introduction to Statistical Quality Control, Wiley, chapter on CUSUM and EWMA charts.

      target and σ are the in-control mean and standard deviation of the series, and they are yours to supply: estimate them from a stretch you know to be normal (Welford does that), not from the stretch under test. The chart assumes independent samples; a strongly autocorrelated series inflates the spread of z_t beyond what the limits allow for and signals too often.

      The zero value is not usable; build one with NewEWMAChart or DefaultEWMAChart. Safe to copy, not safe for concurrent use. NaN and ±Inf are skipped and counted in Skipped, and do not advance t. Finite inputs within a factor of a few of the float64 limit can overflow z_t; rescale such a series first.

      Defined in drift/chart.go.

      PageHinkley 

      type PageHinkley struct

      PageHinkley is the Page–Hinkley test for a change in the mean of a series whose level is not known in advance.

      It is CUSUM with the target replaced by the running mean of everything seen since the last Reset. With mean_t the mean of x_1..x_t, it keeps

      m⁺_t = Σ (x_i − mean_i − δ), M⁺_t = min(0, m⁺_1, …, m⁺_t) m⁻_t = Σ (x_i − mean_i + δ), M⁻_t = max(0, m⁻_1, …, m⁻_t)

      and signals Up when m⁺_t − M⁺_t > λ and Down when M⁻_t − m⁻_t > λ (the larger when both, Up on a tie). δ plays the part of CUSUM’s k, the size of deviation tolerated without accumulating evidence, and λ the part of h.

      Use it when there is no target to supply: a series per process, per disk, per tenant, each settling at its own level. The price is that the reference level is learned from the series itself. Shortly after Reset it rests on a handful of samples, so the test is noisier than CUSUM with a true target. After a change the running mean slowly absorbs the new level, the evidence stops growing and then drains, and the next change would be measured against a blend of both regimes. Reset after acting on a signal. When the target is known, CUSUM is the sharper test.

      Method: E. S. Page, “Continuous inspection schemes”, Biometrika 41(1/2), 1954; D. V. Hinkley, “Inference about the change-point from cumulative sum tests”, Biometrika 58(3), 1971. The running-mean formulation is the one given by J. Gama, I. Žliobaitė, A. Bifet, M. Pechenizkiy and A. Bouchachia, “A survey on concept drift adaptation”, ACM Computing Surveys 46(4), 2014.

      δ and λ are in the data’s units and the caller’s to choose. There is no literature default worth the name, since both scale with the noise. δ = σ/2 and λ = 5σ, the CUSUM defaults, are a reasonable start: at those values the in-control average run length measured here is about 480 samples, close to CUSUM’s 465, and a one-sigma shift after 100 in-control samples is detected in about 11 to 12 (TestPageHinkleyARL). The delay grows when the shift comes early, while the mean still rests on few samples.

      The zero value is not usable; build one with NewPageHinkley. Safe to copy, not safe for concurrent use. NaN and ±Inf are skipped and counted in Skipped. Finite inputs within a factor of a few of the float64 limit can overflow the sums; rescale such a series first.

      Defined in drift/pagehinkley.go.

      Signal 

      type Signal int

      Signal is what a detector says about the sample it was just given.

      It is a statistic crossing a threshold the caller chose, not a probability and not a verdict: Up means “the evidence for an upward shift is now larger than you said it had to be”, and what to do about that is the caller’s.

      Defined in drift/signal.go.

      Welford 

      type Welford struct

      Welford is a running mean and variance in one pass and fixed memory.

      The obvious one-pass method keeps Σx and Σx² and takes (Σx² − (Σx)²/n)/(n−1) at the end. On data far from zero that subtracts two nearly equal huge numbers, and the variance it returns is mostly rounding: readings that move by a few units around 1e9 lose every significant digit of their spread, and the result can even come out negative. Welford’s update keeps the mean and the sum of squared deviations from it instead, so nothing large is ever subtracted from anything large.

      Method: B. P. Welford, “Note on a method for calculating corrected sums of squares and products”, Technometrics 4(3), 1962. Merge is the pairwise combination of T. F. Chan, G. H. Golub and R. J. LeVeque, “Updating formulae and a pairwise algorithm for computing sample variances”, Stanford report STAN-CS-79-773, 1979, published as “Algorithms for computing the sample variance: analysis and recommendations”, The American Statistician 37(3), 1983.

      The zero value is an empty accumulator, ready to use. A Welford is a plain value and safe to copy, which is how a snapshot is taken; it is not safe for concurrent use. NaN and ±Inf are skipped and counted in Skipped, never folded in: one NaN would otherwise poison the mean for the rest of the series. Finite values whose squared spread overflows float64 (beyond about 1e154) give a variance of +Inf, never NaN or a negative number; the mean stays finite. Rescale such data first if the variance matters.

      Defined in drift/welford.go.

      CUSUM.Since 

      func (c *CUSUM) Since() int64

      Since is the number of consecutive samples, ending with the latest, for which the leading side has been above zero: an estimate of how many samples ago the shift began, so it started at sample N() − Since() + 1.

      The leading side is the larger of S⁺ and S⁻, Up on a tie. That is always the side Add just signalled, if it signalled: a side over h is larger than one that is not, and when both are over, Add picks the larger too. Since is 0 while both sides are at zero.

      Defined in drift/cusum.go.

      DefaultCUSUM 

      func DefaultCUSUM(target, sigma float64) (CUSUM, error)

      DefaultCUSUM returns NewCUSUM(target, 0.5·sigma, 5·sigma) for a series with in-control standard deviation sigma > 0.

      Montgomery recommends k = σ/2, which tunes the test for a one-sigma shift, with h = 4σ or h = 5σ. Two-sided, these give an in-control average run length of about 168 samples at h = 4 and about 465 at h = 5, against about 8.4 and 10.4 samples to detect a one-sigma shift. 5 is the default because it roughly triples the time between false alarms for two extra samples of delay; on a series read every second, h = 4 would raise a false alarm about every three minutes per series. TestCUSUMARL measures both run lengths.

      Defined in drift/cusum.go.

      DefaultEWMAChart 

      func DefaultEWMAChart(target, sigma float64) (EWMAChart, error)

      DefaultEWMAChart returns NewEWMAChart(target, sigma, 0.2, 3).

      These are the literature’s working values, not a tuning for your data. Montgomery recommends λ between 0.05 and 0.25, and reports that L = 3, the usual three-sigma width, works well, particularly with the larger λ. Lucas and Saccucci’s tables put the limit for an in-control average run length of 500 at λ = 0.2 slightly inside 3 (L ≈ 2.96 for the asymptotic band); 3 is kept because it is the convention readers recognise and it errs towards fewer false alarms. Measured here with the exact, time-varying band, the in-control average run length at these values is about 560 samples, and a one-sigma shift is detected in about 10 (TestEWMAChartARL).

      Defined in drift/chart.go.
